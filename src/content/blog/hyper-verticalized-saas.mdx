## Most B2B Software Is Generic by Necessity

For most of the last 20 years, the SaaS model worked like this: build once, sell to many. The economics required it. Custom software was expensive. The more a vendor could standardize their product, the more customers they could serve without proportionally growing their engineering team.

That constraint shaped everything. Most B2B buyers learned to accept software that almost fit. They hired consultants to bridge the gap. They built spreadsheets to handle edge cases. They paid for features they didn't use and worked around the ones they needed.

## A Framework for Thinking About It

Two axes matter when evaluating business software:

- **Bespoke vs. Productized:** Built specifically for this customer, or off-the-shelf?
- **Flexible vs. Guided:** Does it enforce a workflow, or leave structure to the user?

Most enterprise SaaS lives in the bottom-right: Workflow Software. Productized and guided. Shopify, Salesforce, industry-specific ERPs. These are the systems that run most businesses.

The top-right quadrant — Adaptive Systems — is what every sophisticated buyer actually wants. Software that enforces structured workflows but is built specifically for how their business operates. Historically, only the largest enterprises could afford it. Everyone else compromised.

## The Precursor: Low-Code Tools

The demand was always there. Retool and Airtable took this problem on.

At a $50M ARR manufacturing marketplace, we replaced purpose-built SaaS tools with Retool and Airtable workflows. Not because we preferred them. Because the off-the-shelf options didn't fit, and these let us build something closer to what we actually needed without a full engineering cycle.

The result was clumsy. Retool requires a developer. Airtable breaks at scale. The UX is obviously stitched together. But we got workflows that matched our operations instead of the reverse.

That's the demand signal. Companies will put up with clumsy tools to get something that fits. The question was always whether something cleaner was possible.

## AI Changes the Build Economics

The cost to move from productized to bespoke has dropped sharply. A small team can now build a guided, workflow-enforcing system tailored to a specific customer's operations at a fraction of what it cost three years ago.

This doesn't mean SaaS is going away. It means the category is fragmenting. Buyers who accepted generic tools because bespoke was unaffordable now have options. Builders who needed large engineering teams to serve them don't.

The result is hyper-verticalization: software built for smaller and smaller customer segments, with tighter structural fit to how each segment actually operates. In the limit, personalization for each customer.

## My Test

I'm building ERPForge to test this directly. The hypothesis: an AI-assisted ERP that adapts to a specific company's operations, at a build cost that makes it viable for small and mid-sized manufacturers.

Retool and Airtable took this problem on. The question is whether AI gets the rest of the way — where the new floor on build cost lands, and whether the resulting product is structurally strong enough to support real operations or just a cleaner version of the same clumsy workaround.
